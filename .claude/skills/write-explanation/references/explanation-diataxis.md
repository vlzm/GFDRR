# Explanation

Explanation is a discursive treatment of a subject that permits *reflection*.
Explanation is **understanding-oriented**.

---

Explanation deepens and widens the reader's understanding of a subject. It
brings clarity, light, and context.

The idea of *reflection* is important. Reflection happens *after* something
else, and depends on that something else, yet it also brings something new — it
sheds new light on the subject.

The perspective of explanation is higher and wider than that of the other three
document types. It does not look through the user's eyes, as a how-to guide
does, and it does not give a close-up of the mechanism, as reference does. In
each case its field is the topic, the "area of knowledge", which somehow has to
be bounded in a reasonable, considered way.

For the user, explanation ties things together. It is the answer to the
question: *Tell me about…?*

It is the kind of documentation that makes sense to read away from the product
itself. (You could say explanation is the only kind of documentation it might
make sense to read in the bath.)

---

## The value and place of explanation

### Explanation and understanding

Explanation is set apart by its distance from the practitioner's immediate
concerns. It has no direct consequence for what they are doing or for their
work. Because of this it is sometimes treated as less important. That is a
mistake: it may be less *urgent* than the other three types, but it is no less
*important*. It is not a luxury. No practitioner of a craft can do without an
understanding of that craft, and they need explanatory material to help weave
that understanding together.

**Explanation by another name**

Your explanatory documentation need not be called *"Explanation"*. Alternatives
include:

- *Discussion*
- *Background / context*
- *Concept guides*
- *Topics*

The word *"explanation"* — and its equivalents in other languages — means
"unfolding", the disclosure of what is hidden in the folds. So explanation
brings into the light what was implicit or hidden.

Similarly, words meaning "understanding" often share a root with words meaning
"to hold" or "to grasp" (as in "to grasp an idea"). This is an important part of
understanding — to be able to hold something or possess it. Understanding binds
together the other components of our command of a craft and makes it reliably
ours.

Understanding does not *arise from* explanation, but explanation is needed to
form the web that helps hold everything together. Without it, a practitioner's
knowledge of their craft is loose, scattered, and fragile, and its application
is *anxious*.

### Explanation and its boundaries

Quite often explanation is not distinguished explicitly in documentation, and
the very idea that something needs explaining is expressed only vaguely.
Instead, explanation is usually scattered in small fragments across other
sections.

Writing good explanatory material is not always easy. Where do you start? It is
not always clear where to stop either. There is an openness to it that can give
the author too many possibilities.

Tutorials, how-to guides, and reference are all clearly defined in their scope
by something that is itself well defined: what you need to teach the user, what
task the user needs to solve, or simply the bounds of the mechanism/system
itself.

In the case of explanation it helps to have a real or imagined "why" question to
serve as a starting point. Otherwise you simply have to draw the boundaries of a
reasonable field yourself, and be content with that.

## How to write good explanation

### Make connections

When you write explanation, you help weave a web of understanding for your
readers. **Make connections** to other things, even ones beyond the immediate
topic, if it helps.

### Provide context

**Give background and context in your explanation**: explain *why* things are
the way they are — design decisions, historical reasons, technical constraints —
draw conclusions, and give concrete examples.

### Talk *about* the subject

**Worth discussing:**

- the bigger picture
- history
- choices, alternatives, possibilities
- why: reasons and justifications

Explanatory guides are about a topic in the sense that they stand *around* it.
Even the titles of your explanatory guides should reflect this; before each
heading it should be possible to place — mentally, or even explicitly — the word
"about" (for example, "About…"). For instance: *"About user authentication"* or
*"About database connection policies"*.

### Admit opinion and perspective

Opinion may seem a strange thing to bring into documentation. But the fact is
that all human activity and knowledge are shot through with opinion, belief, and
thought. The reality of any human creation is saturated with opinion, and that
must be part of any understanding of that creation.

In the same way, any understanding proceeds from some point of view, a
particular position — which means other points of view and positions exist too.
**Explanation can and should consider alternatives**, counter-examples, or
several different approaches to the same question.

In explanation you are not giving instructions or describing facts — you are
opening the topic up for consideration. It helps to think of explanation as a
discussion: in a discussion you can consider and weigh even conflicting
*opinions*.

### Keep explanation within clear bounds

One of the risks of explanation is that it tends to absorb other things. The
author, striving to cover the topic fully, feels the urge to include
instructions or technical description related to it. But documentation already
has other places for those, and letting them leak in here hurts the explanation
itself and removes them from where they ought to be seen.

## The language of explanation

*"The reason for x is that historically y came about…"*
Explain.

*"W is better than z, because…"*
Offer judgments, and even opinions, where it is appropriate.

*"An x in system y is analogous to a w in system z. However…"*
Give context that helps the reader.

*"Some users prefer w (because z). This can be a good approach, but…"*
Weigh alternatives.

*"An x interacts with a y as follows: …"*
Reveal the inner workings of the mechanism, to help the reader see why something
works the way it does.

## An analogy from food and cooking

In 1984 Harold McGee published *On Food and Cooking*.

The book does not teach you to cook anything. It has no recipes (except as
historical examples), and it is not a reference work. Instead it places food and
cooking in the context of history, society, science, and technology. It
explains, for example, why we do what we do in the kitchen, and how that has
changed over time.

Clearly it is not a book we would read *while* cooking. We read it when we want
to reflect on cooking. It illuminates the subject by looking at it from
different points of view, shedding light from different angles.

Having read a book like *On Food and Cooking*, we change our understanding. Our
knowledge becomes richer and deeper. What we learned may not be immediately
applicable the next time we do something in the kitchen, but *it will change how
we think about our craft, and it will influence our practice*.
